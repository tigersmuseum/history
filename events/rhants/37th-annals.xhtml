Annals of the 37th Regiment

INTRODUCTION.

I HAVE endeavoured in the following pages to supply the deficiency often felt of a correct record of the 37th North Hampshire Regiment.

With this object I have stated, without embellishment, and, as far as possible, in chronological order, the leading incidents connected with the services of the Regiment since its enrolment in 1702.

I have collected my materials from various sources. Besides the information to be found in the official "Digest of Service," I have derived assistance from the records of the services of those Regiments with which the 37th has at various times been associated.

I am also indebted to the valuable pamphlet entitled "Presentation of Colours to the 37th Regiment," published in 1865, by the late Surgeon-Major J. W. Fleming, F.R.C.S.

I submit this publication, not unconscious of its imperfections, but in the hope that it may be useful and acceptable to the Regiment.

JOHN EVERARD WHITTING, Winchester, April, 1878. Captain, 37th Regiment.

Annals of the 37th Regiment.

1702.

The 37th North Hampshire Regiment was one of the newly-formed corps which were called into existence after the formal declaration of war against France in May, 1702, when it was found necessary to increase the military establishment of the country. It was raised in Ireland, and the Colonelcy was conferred on Thomas Meredith, then a Captain in the 3rd Horse, now and Dragoon Guards.

Within the first few years after its enrolment, the Regiment was destined to play an important part in the brilliant career of victories which attended the British arms under Marlborough.

1703.

In May, 1703, the Regiment embarked for Holland, being part of the sixteen thousand English troops with which the Duke of Marlborough commenced his celebrated march on the 19th of that month, and was employed with the covering army during the sieges of Huy and Limburg.

1704.

The following year Marlborough changed the scene of his operations, and marching through Coblentz into Germany, crossed the Neckar, and on the 2nd of July defeated the Bavarian army in the fortified position of Schellenburg, near Donawert, in which action the 37th Regiment was engaged.

On the 13th of August, the Regiment took part in the battle of Blenheim, fought by the allied armies under Marlborough and Prince Eugene, against the superior numbers of the French, under Tallard. Previous to the action, Marlborough addressed the officers in the following terms :-

" I know the danger, yet a battle is absolutely necessary, and I rely on the bravery and discipline of the troops, which will make amends for our disadvantages." His confidence was not misplaced. Tallard, with many other officers, was taken prisoner, and the whole garrison of Blenheim, consisting of 26 battalions of infantry and 12 squadrons of cavalry laid down their arms.

This victory completely established the military ascendancy of the allied armies, and delivered Germany from France.

The Regiment was subsequently sent in charge of prisoners to Holland.

1705.

In the spring of the following year the Regiment served in the expedition to the Moselle; and on the night of the 17th July, formed part of the main body which attacked and forced the fortified lines at Helixem and Neer-Hespen.

1706.

In May, 1706, the 37th Regiment was engaged in the memorable victory of Ramilies, and was also present at the siege of Ostend, which surrendered in the beginning of July.

1707-8.

The campaign of 1707 was not marked by any important engagement, but in the following year the Regiment gained especial distinction at the battle of Oudenarde, on the 11th July, in which action it formed the leading brigade with the 8th, 18th, and 23rd Regiments, under the command of General Sabine.

After serving with the covering army during the siege of Lisle, the Regiment passed the winter of 1708-9 at Ghent.

1709.

In July, 1709, the Regiment was engaged in the siege of Tournay, which surrendered on the 31st August. It was present at the sanguinary battle of Malplaquet, on the 11th September. In September and October it was employed in covering the siege of Mons, and returned to its former winter quarters at Ghent.

1710.

The campaign of 1710 comprised the seiges of Douay (which surrendered on the 27th June), Bethune (which surrendered on the 29th August), and of Aire and St. Venant; in all of which the 37th formed part of the covering army.

1711.

In 1711 the Regiment returned to England, and was shortly afterwards despatched with the expedition against Quebec, under Brigadier General Hill. Colonel Windress was in command of the Regiment at the time. The transports, however, in which the 4th, 37th, and two other Regiments had embarked were wrecked in the St. Lawrence river, and in consequence of this disaster the expedition was abandoned. One major, one captain, five lieutenants, one ensign, 14 sergeants, 8 drummers, and 231 rank and file of the Regiment were drowned.

1712.

On its return to England, the Regiment was completed by drafts from other corps, and in 1712 once more embarked for Flanders, to join the army under the Duke of Ormond.

Upon the cessation of hostilities in this year, the 37th returned home, and were soon afterwards quartered in Ireland, where it appears they remained for several years.

1742.

In the summer of 1742, at the outbreak of the war of the Austrian Succession, the 37th Regiment, commanded by Colonel Ponsonby, embarked for the Netherlands, and joined the allied Anglo-Hanoverian army under Field-Marshal the Earl of Stair.

1743.

Early in 1743 this army commenced its march for Germany, and in June was commanded by the King of England, George II, in person, and H.R.H. the Duke of Cumberland.

The chief event of the campaign was the defeat of the French at Dettingen, on the 27th June. The engagement was notable as being the last occasion on which an English monarch took command on a field of battle.

1744-5.

The Regiment served the campaign of 1744 under Field-Marshal Wade, and remained abroad till the autumn of 1745, when it was brought to England in consequence of the rebellion in Scotland.

1746.

On the 17th January, 1746, the Regiment was engaged at the battle of Falkirk, in which their Colonel, Sir Robert Monro, was killed. The action was fought under a terrific storm of wind and rain, and other adverse circumstances.

On the 17th of April in the same year, the 37th Regiment, commanded by Colonel Dejean, with a total strength of 23 officers, and 468 men, took part in the battle of Culloden, which resulted in the utter collapse of the Pretender Charles Edward. In this action the 37th had 14 men killed, 5 officers and 68 men wounded.

1747.

In 1747, the 37th Regiment was again ordered to Holland in conjunction with other troops. A brigade composed of the 13th, 25th, and 37th Regiments occupying the Village of Val, near Maestricht, was attacked on the 2nd July by the French, and after a most obstinate resistance, was at length compelled to withdraw before overwhelming numbers.

1748-9.

The treaty of peace concluded at Aix-la-Chapelle, on the 7th October, 1748, put an end to hostilities, and the Regiment proceeded to the Island of Minorca in 1749, where it appears to have remained till its return to England in 1755.

1751.

In the meantime the King's Warrant had been issued for regulating the colours and clothing of the marching Regiments of Foot, in accordance with which "Bright Yellow" was ordered as the distinguishing colour of the facings of the 37th Regiment. The text of this Warrant, so far as regards Infantry Regiments, is as follows :-

"GEORGE R. - Our will and pleasure is that the following Regulations for the Colours, Clothing, &c., of our marching Regiments of Foot, and for the Uniform Clothing of our Cavalry, their Standards, Guidons, Banners, &c., be duly observed and put in execution at such times as these particulars are, or shall be furnished, viz :-Regulations for the Colours, Clothing, &c., of the marching Regiments of Foot :-

"No Colonel to put his arms, breast device, or livery, on any part of the appointments of the Regiment under his command.

"COLOURS. - The King's or first colour of every Regiment is to be the Great Union throughout.

"The second colour to be the colour of the Facings of the Regiment, with the Union in the upper canton, except those Regiments which are faced with Red or White, whose second colour is to be the Red Cross of St. George, in a White Field, and the Union in the upper canton.

"In the centre of each colour is to be painted or embroidered in Gold Roman Characters, the number of the Rank of the Regiment, within a wreath of roses and thistles on the same stalk, except those Regiments which are allowed to wear any Royal Devices, or ancient Badges, on whose colours the Rank of the Regiment is to be painted towards the upper corner.

"The size of the colours and the length of the Pike to be the same as those of the Royal Regiments of Foot Guards. "The cords and tassels of all colours to be Crimson and Gold mixed.

"DRUMMERS' CLOTHING. - The Drummers of all the Royal Regiments are allowed to wear the Royal Livery, viz:-Red, lined, faced, and lapelled on the breast with blue, and laced with a Royal lace. The Drummers of all the other Regiments are to be clothed with the colour of the Facings of their Regiments, lined, faced, and lapelled on the breast with red, and laced in such a manner as the Colonel shall think fit for distinction sake, the lace, however, being of the colours of that on the soldiers' coats.

"DRUMS.-The front or fore-part of the drums to be painted with the colour of the Facings of the Regiment, with the King's Cypher and Crown, and the number of the Regiment under it. "CAMP COLOURS. -The Camp colours to be square, and of the colour of the Facings of the Regiment, with the number of the Regiment upon them." * * * * * * *

(The Warrant closes with a particular statement, and an abstract of the Facings of the fifty Infantry Regiments).

"Given at our Court, at Kensington, this the 1st day of July, 1751, in the 25th year of Our Reign. "By His Majesty's command, "(Signed) H. Fox."

1756.

In 1756 orders were issued for the formation of second battalions to fifteen Regiments, including the 37th, but in 1758 these battalions were formed into separate Regiments, and the second battalion of the 37th became the 75th; it was however eventually disbanded with other Regiments after the peace of Fontainebleau, in 1763.

1758.

In 1758 the 37th Regiment was ordered to Germany.

1759.

Early the following year hostilities were resumed. The British contingent comprised 22,000 men.

The chief feature of the campaign was the Battle of Minden in Westphalia on the 1st August. The six Regiments which formed the British force in this engagement were the 12th, 20th, 23rd, 25th, 37th, and 51st Regiments, of which the 12th, 23rd, and 37th formed the leading brigade.

The following reference to the action occurs in the Annual Register :-

"The brunt of the battle was almost wholly sustained by the British Infantry and some corps of Hanoverians, who stood the reiterated charges of so many bodies of horse, the strength and glories of the French armies, with a resolution, steadiness, and expertness in their manœuvres which was never excelled, perhaps never equalled. They cut to pieces or entirely routed those bodies. Two brigades of foot attempted to support them, but they vanished before the English Infantry."

In consequence of this victory Royal Authority was granted to the 37th Regiment to bear "MINDEN" on their colours.

1760.

The Regiment served in the campaign of 1760 under Prince Ferdinand of Brunswick.

1761.

In June, 1761, the Regiment was brigaded with the 5th, 12th, and 24th Regiments, under command of Brigadier General Sandford in the Marquis of Granby's division, and was employed in several minor operations. After undergoing severe hardships, the brigade was stationed near the village of Kirch-Denkern near to Fellinghausen. On the 15th July the position was unsuccessfully attacked by the French the engagement lasting through the night and the following day.

1762.

The 37th Regiment, brigaded with the same regiments as in the preceding year, was engaged on the 24th June at the Battle of Groebenstein, where the French army was surprised, and leaving their tents and equipage at the mercy of the victors, retreated in the direction of Cassel.

1763.

In 1763 peace was proclaimed, and the thanks of Parliament were communicated to the army for its conduct during the war.

The 37th, and five other Regiments, (viz:-the 11th and 33rd from Germany, the 3rd and 67th from Portugal, and the 57th from Gibraltar) - were sent to form the Garrison of Minorca.

This island, after its capture during the war by the French had again been restored to Great Britain under the provisions of Peace.

1769.

The Regiment remained in Minorca till 1769, and in 1774 proceeded to Ireland.

1776.

In 1776 the 37th Regiment embarked for America, and in the early part of July, with the 10th, 38th, and 52nd Regiments, formed the third brigade of the Army under Major-General Jones, in the division commanded by Lieut.-General Earl Percy.

The 37th Regiment took part in the capture of Long Island on the 26th of August in this year, and served in subsequent operations during the whole of the American War.

1782.

After the peace of 1782, the Regiment proceeded to Nova Scotia and Newfoundland.

At this time the 37th first received its title of "The North Hampshire Regiment."

The following is a copy of the Circular addressed to the Colonel of the Regiment by the Commander-in-Chief :-

"London, 31st August, 1782. "His Majesty having been pleased to order that the 37th Regiment of Foot, which you command, should take the County name of the 37th or North Hampshire Regiment, and be looked upon as attached to that division of the county, I am to acquaint you it is His Majesty's further pleasure that you should in all things conform to that idea, and endeavour by all means in your power to cultivate and improve that connection, so as to create a mutual attachment between the county and the Regiment, which may at all times be useful towards recruiting the Regiment. But as the completing of the several Regiments, now generally so deficient, is in the present crisis of the most important national concern, you will on this occasion use the utmost possible exertion for that purpose, by prescribing the greatest diligence to your officers and recruiting parties, and by every suitable attention to the gentlemen and considerable inhabitants; and as nothing can so much tend to conciliate their affections as an orderly and polite behaviour towards them, and an observance of the strictest discipline in all your quarters, you will give the most positive orders on that head, and you will immediately make such a disposition of your recruiting parties, as may best answer that end.

"I have, &c., "(Signed) H. S. CONWAY."

1789.

In 1789 the Regiment returned to England, but did not long enjoy the repose of home service.

1793.

On the 14th March, 1793, the 37th embarked for Holland, and in the following month the flank companies of the 14th, 37th, and 53rd Regiments placed under the orders of Major Matthews, of the 53rd Regiment, were despatched to Marquain, on the borders of French Flanders, to watch the enemy, in which service they were employed till the 20th May.

The Duke of York took command of the British and Hanoverian troops in Flanders, and co-operated with the Austrians under the Prince of Saxe-Coburg.

On the 23rd May, the troops attacked the enemy's fortified camp at Famars, and so over-powered them that the French retreated behind the Scheldt. The action is thus mentioned in the London Gazette:-

"The British troops, who had this opportunity of distinguishing themselves, were the brigade of the line, viz:-the 14th, 37th, and 53rd Regiments, with the battalion formed from the Light Infantry and Grenadier Companies, under the command of Major-General Abercromby; seven pieces of cannon and two hundred prisoners were taken in the redoubts."

The allied army then invested the fortress of Valenciennes, which capitulated on the 29th July.

After the surrender of this town, the British troops marched towards Cambray, and eventually undertook the siege of Dunkirk. Here however, the French had been allowed to amass immense bodies of troops; they made various sorties from Dunkirk in great strength. One sortie made by the garrison on the 8th September, was repulsed by the 14th and 37th Regiments, who were cheered for their gallantry by the Austrian corps under Joseph Coloredo, as they passed to the front.

1794.

The Army assembled in April, 1794, on the heights of Cateau, where it was reviewed by the Emperor of Germany. The 14th, 37th, and 53rd Regiments were again brigaded together, under command of H.R.H. William Frederick Duke of Gloucester.

The 37th Regiment was engaged at the Battle of Monoran, on the 17th and 18th May, as appears from the Duke of York's Despatches, dated Tournay, 19th May, 1794 [1], when Lieutenants Murray and Cunningham were wounded, and Captain Cooke and Lieutenant Mc Kenzie were missing.

The 37th Regiment most highly distinguished itself in the action fought near Tournay, on the 22nd May, and in consequence "TOURNAY" was ordered to be inscribed on the colours of the Regiment. The conduct of the brigade was commended by H.R.H. the Duke of York. The following record appears in Captain Jones' Journal :-

"Major-General Fox, with the 14th, 37th, and 53rd Regiments was engaged with the whole of the French columns which had marched from Lisle, and the different corps which had driven the rest of the army back, fell upon its flanks and rear; perhaps there is not on record a single instance of greater gallantry or more soldier-like conduct than was exhibited on that day by these three Regiments. At length General Fox, finding that the whole army had left him, began to think of retreating, to effect which it was necessary to get possession of the causeway leading to Leers; but before that could be accomplished he was obliged to charge several battalions of the enemy, who were astonished that such a handful of men should presume to give them battle, and expected every moment that they would lay down their arms, but with a degree of intrepidity that words cannot describe, and is indeed scarcely conceivable, they gained the wished-for point, and then formed with such regularity, that the enemy could not assail them; they secured their retreat towards Leers, and the next morning joined General Otto's column."

The following was the general order published under date, Tournay, May 23rd, 1794:-

"His Royal Highness the Commander-in-Chief desires to express his most particular thanks to Major-General Fox, to the 14th Regiment, under the command of Lieutenant Colonel Ramsay, to the 37th Regiment, commanded by Captain Lightburne, to the 53rd Regiment, commanded by Major Wiseman, and to the detachment of Artillery attached to them, under the command of Captain Trotter, for that display of intrepidity and good conduct, which reflects the greatest honour upon themselves, at the same time that it was highly instrumental in deciding the important victory of the 22nd instant. His Royal Highness much laments the loss they have sustained, but flatters himself they feel it in some measure compensated by the credit they have gained."

In his public despatch, the Duke of York speaking of the British troops engaged, stated :-

"Nothing could exceed the spirit and gallantry with which they conducted themselves, particularly in the storm of the village of Pontechin, which they forced with the bayonet."

At this village the Regiment suffered considerable loss, and Captain Spread, Lieutenants Mitchell and Mc Lean were wounded.

The 37th Regiment again distinguished itself on the 20th October, 1794, near Nimeguen. This action, which was witnessed by General Sir Alexander Duff, is thus described in one of the Duke of York's despatches :-

"Head Quarters, Nimeguen, October 20th, 1794.

"SIR, -Yesterday morning, the enemy attacked the whole of the advanced posts of my right wing in very great force, particularly that of Druten, which was defended by the 37th Regiment, and that of Appethen, where the Prince of Rohan's light battalion was posted; nothing could exceed the gallantry with which the whole of the troops of advanced posts (particularly those of two corps) behaved, but at last the post on the left of the 37th Regiment, which was occupied by a detachment of Rohan's Hussars, having been forced, Major Hope, who commanded the 37th Regiment, and who distinguished himself exceedingly, was obliged to retreat upon the dyke along the Waal, which he continued for some time without being much annoyed by the enemy; unfortunately, however, a strong body of the enemy's Hussars being mistaken for the corps of Rohan, the regiment allowed them to come upon them unmolested, when the Hussars immediately attacked, and the narrowness of the dyke, which on every other occasion must have afforded a security to the infantry, in this instance acted against them, as they were driven off it by the enemy's charge, and, I am sorry to say, have suffered very considerably. As they were upon the advanced posts, I have not, as yet, received an exact return of their loss, but I understand that Captains Baird, Hendley, and Duff, Lieutenants Mitchell, Thompson, Calgahann, and Murray, with the Quarter-Master, Mr. Duxhall, were made prisoners, of which number Captain Duff, Lieutenants Mitchell and Calgahann were wounded: all the prisoners who were taken agreed in the intelligence that the enemy had brought over thirty thousand men.
I am, &c., (Signed) FREDERICK."

"P.S.-I am sorry to add, that Lieutenant Wadman, of the Light Infantry of the 37th Regiment, who was upon picquet, and who distinguished himself by his intrepidity and prudence, is also dangerously wounded, and was brought in the evening to this town."

After this the Regiment was brigaded with the 8th, 44th, 57th, and 88th Regiments, under Major-General de Burgh, and was stationed near the Waal to defend the passage of that river. The winter was exceptionally severe, and the operations were attended with great distress and hardship to the troops, who finally evacuated Holland and retired into Germany.

1795.

In November, 1795, the 37th Regiment embarked for the West Indies; but the fleet with which they sailed was driven back by tempestuous weather; only two companies ever reached their destination, the men of which were drafted into the 44th Regiment; the rest of the Regiment disembarked at different ports, and when concentrated marched to Poole, and thence to Portsmouth.

1796-1800.

On the 10th May, 1796, the 37th embarked for Gibraltar, where it landed on the 16th June and remained till 1800, when it again sailed for the West Indies, and landed in the island of St. Vincent on the 19th April in that year.

1808.

In February, 1808, the Regiment moved to Trinidad for a year, and returned to England in July, 1809, landing at Portsmouth in October.

Thence the Regiment proceeded to Burton-upon-Trent, from Burton-upon-Trent to Derby, from Derby, in 1810, to Horsham, from Horsham to Blatshington, from Blatshington to Lewes, and thence to Portsmouth, where it embarked for Ireland and landed at the Cove of Cork on the 1st January, 1811.

1811.

The Regiment was quartered at Middleton for some months; then, in the following December, re-embarked at Cork and arrived at Hastings in January, 1812, thence proceeded to Portsmouth, embarked in February for Gibraltar, and arrived there in April.

1813.

In April, 1813, the Regiment received an augmentation of four companies, which were formed at Hastings. In July the establishment was augmented to six companies, forming a Second Battalion, which proceeded in October to Battle, and in November to Silverhill Barracks.

This Second Battalion, under command of Lieut.-Colonel Hart, was ordered to Ramsgate, where it embarked on the 10th December, sailed on the 15th for Holland, and landed on the 17th, four companies at Schaveling, and two companies at Helvoetsluys.

The Schaveling detachment proceeded to the Hague, and so on by canal to Rotterdam, and thence to Williamstadt, where it arrived on the 21st December, and was joined by the two companies from Helvoetsluys.

On the 25th December the second battalion was further augmented by four companies, and at once advanced to Staenbergen and Tholen.

1814-16.

On the 2nd and 3rd February, 1814, the battalion was engaged in a sortie made by the French from Antwerp upon the village of Mecham; Lieut. Stewart and Ensign Chapman were wounded, each losing a leg by a cannon shot.

The battalion thence proceeded to Woun and Haaltrean; and on the 8th and 9th March took part in the assault on Bergen-op-Zoom, where the following casualties occurred :-

Ensign Sands, 5 sergeants, and 32 rank and file, killed.
Ensign Stanton, 6 sergeants, and 67 rank and file, wounded.
Five Officers, viz:-Lieut. Dyer (wounded), Ensign and Adjutant Long (wounded), Ensign J. L. Butler, Ensign W. Ralph, and Assistant-Surgeon Stobo, 3 sergeants, 7 drummers, and 121 rank and file were taken prisoners.

In the mean time the First Battalion of the Regiment, under command of Lieut.-Colonel James, having embarked from Gibraltar on the 8th and 10th February, had joined the Peninsular Army under Lord Wellington, and remained with it until the close of the campaign.

On the 4th June, upon the withdrawal of the army from France, this battalion embarked at Bordeaux for North America, reaching Quebec on the 3rd August; they proceeded the same day in two divisions, seven companies by land, and two by steamboats, to Montreal; one company was detained at Quebec under quarantine.

On the 21st August they moved to Kingston, Upper Canada, the company from Quebec joining headquarters on the 9th September.

On the 28th October, the battalion embarked on board the "St. Lawrence,” “Prince Regent," "Montreal," and "Niagara," ships of war, and arrived at Fort George on the 2nd November; thence the Regiment marched to Burlington, where it remained until the 17th June, 1815, when it proceeded to Queenstown. Leaving this on the 14th August, the battalion arrived at Fort Erie two days later, and embarking on the 17th, reached Amherstburg on the 27th August.

On the 23rd June, 1816, under command of Lieut.-Colonel James, this battalion embarked at Amherstburg, and arrived at Fort Erie in four days; thence it marched to Fort George.

On the 2nd September, this battalion under command of Major Cockrane, embarked at Fort George, and arrived at Kingstown on the 6th December, one company being left on detachment at Drummond's Island.

On the 4th May, 1814, the second battalion commanded by Lieut.-Colonel Hart, moved from Oudenbosch and Emmelin (where the prisoners taken at Bergen-op Zoom had re-joined) to Antwerp. On the 5th September they marched out to the village of Contich, near Malines, in consequence of the sickly state of the men, returning to the citadel of Antwerp on the 26th October.

On the 6th of April, 1815, a special letter was written to the Colonel of the Regiment, giving Royal Authority for "PENINSULA" being inscribed on the Colours of the Regiment.

On the 11th February, 1816, the second battalion under command of Captain Walton, left Antwerp, embarked at Ostend on the 23rd, landed at Dover on the 26th, thence proceeded to Ireland, disembarking at Monkstown on the 26th and 29th March; and moved to Cork, and eventually to Fermoy, where it arrived on the 3rd of April. The Depôt under command of Major Burer, joined the Head Quarters here on the 10th May.

1817.

This battalion occupied quarters at Fermoy, with detachments at Ballincolig, Youghal, Buttevant, and Mallow, up to the 24th March, 1817, and obtained the approbation of the General Officer commanding, as well as the thanks and good wishes of the inhabitants for their exemplary conduct during their stay in the neighbourhood.

The general reduction of the Army at this time led to the dismemberment of this battalion, which was carried into effect under the superintendence of Major-General Benjamin Gordon.

Twenty sergeants, 5 drummers, and 450 rank and file were drafted off into the first battalion of the Regiment. The detachment embarked at Cork under command of Major Burer, in May, arrived at Quebec on the 8th July, and proceeded to Kingston, Upper Canada, where it joined Head Quarters.

About the same time the Light Company, which had been left on detachment at Drummond's Island, also rejoined Head Quarters.

1818.

In May, 1818, four companies proceeded from Kingston to La Chine, and relieved the detachment of the late 99th Regiment, in the Montreal district.

Six Companies and Head Quarters left on the 22nd June, and arriving at La Chine (distance 250 miles) on the 24th, marched next day into Montreal.

The 37th was garrisoned here for three years, occupying the posts of La Cole, Isle Aux Noix, St. John's, Chambly, Blairfindell, William Henry, Island of St. Helens, and La Chine.

1821.

In May, 1821, the regiment proceeded in steamboats to Quebec, and remained there three years.

1824.

On the 1st June, 1824, four Companies (2, 3, 4, 5), under command of Major Valiant embarked on board the "Lady Sherbrooke" steamboat for Kingston, where they arrived on the 14th. On the 22nd, the Head Quarters, comprising the Grenadiers, 1st, 6th, and Light Companies, under Lieut.-Colonel Burer, who was now in command of the Regiment, embarked and reached Kingston on the 4th July.

1825.

By a general order, under date Horse Guards, 26th April, 1825, the 37th Regiment received an augmentation of its establishment from eight to ten Companies, six for foreign service, and four as a depôt at home, from which those on foreign service could be recruited.

In October of this year the Regiment was relieved by the 68th Light Infantry; the first division consisting of the Grenadiers, Ist and Light Companies embarking from Kingston in bateaux on the 12th, reached Quebec on the 20th.

The second division consisting of the 2nd, 3rd, and 4th Companies under command of Major Bruce left Kingston on the 19th, and arrived at Quebec on the 27th.

Orders were received to embark for England: upon the occasion of their departure, Lieut.-General Lord Dalhousie, K.C.B., Commander of the Forces in Canada, was pleased to take leave of the Regiment, and issued the following general order, under date Quebec, 28th October, 1825 :-

"General Order. The Commander of the Forces has great pleasure in conveying to Lieut.-Colonel Burer an expression of the high opinion he entertains of the 37th Regiment on its embarkation to return from Canada to England.

Some unpleasant circumstances having occurred within these last few years, Lord Dalhousie adverts to them on this occasion only for the purpose of recording in General Orders that while these circumstances did exist, they never interfered with the discipline or soldier-like conduct and appearance of the Regiment; but these circumstances he hopes and believes have now passed away, and the Commander of the Forces feels on the departure of the 37th Regiment the loss of one of the best disciplined and most efficient Regiments under his command. Such are the terms in which he shall report to his Royal Highness the Commander in Chief.

Lord Dalhousie has only to assure Lieut.-Colonel Burer, the Officers, and Non-Commissioned Officers and Soldiers of the 37th Regiment that they carry with them his cordial good-wishes for the happiness and honour of the Regiment wherever it may be called to serve."

1826.

The Regiment landed at Portsmouth in December, and remained there till September in the following year, when it crossed over to Ireland.

From the following letter it appears that the addition of the name "Tournay" to the colours was finally authorised in this year.

"Horse Guards, 18th July, 1826.
Sir, I have the honour to acquaint you, by direction of His Royal Highness the Commander in Chief, that His Majesty has been pleased to approve of the 37th Regiment bearing on its colours and appointments, in addition to any other badges or devices which may heretofore have been granted to the Regiment, the word "Tournay" in commemoration of the distinguished conduct of the Regiment in the action fought near Tournay on the 22nd of May, 1794.
I have, &c., (Signed) H. TORRENS, A.G. Officer Commanding 37th Regiment."

The Regiment remained four years in Ireland: the first two years were passed in the county of Limerick, during which time Major-General Sir C. Doyle, K.C.H., was pleased to express his "approbation of the appearance of the Regiment, and its interior economy."

1828.

In 1828 detachments were sent out in aid of the civil power to Ennis and Clare during a warmly contested election for the County Member. The election extended over three days, from the 29th June to the 2nd July. The candidates were Vesey Fitzgerald, Esq., and Daniel O'Connell, Esq.

The Regiment was afterwards moved to Mullingar, thence to Enniskillen, Sligo, and Omagh.

1830.

On the 20th September, 1830, the Regiment was under orders to embark for Bermuda, which they reached in November. They sailed in two divisions, the first consisting of three Companies under command of Major Kell, the remainder commanded by Colonel Smelt.

1832.

On the 24th October, 1832, despatches were received from the Earl of Mulgrave, Governor of Jamaica, by the Commander of the Forces in Bermuda, suggesting that a Regiment might be immediately sent as a reinforcement to check the negroes who were shewing a disposition to rebel. In consequence of these despatches two Companies embarked on the following morning on board H.M.S. Fly, Commander Drake, and arrived at Port Royal on the 5th November. The remainder of the Regiment, with Head-Quarters, followed on the 1st of November in H.M.S. Winchester; Vice-Admiral Sir Griffith Colpoys, under whose flag the ship sailed, was left sick at Bermuda.

The Regiment remained nearly seven years in Jamaica.

1839.

On the 22nd March, 1839, the whole Regiment embarked at Port Royal on Board H.M.S. Hercules (74) Capt. Barnard, for Halifax, Nova Scotia, remaining there three years till its departure for home service on the 28th December, 1841, under command of Major Skelly.

1841.

On this occasion the following complimentary order was issued :-

"Head-Quarters, Halifax, December 24th, 1841.
General Order. The 37th Regiment will embark on board the Abercrombie, Robinson, transport, for Cork, on Tuesday morning next, at nine o'clock, before the men have breakfast, from the dock-yard. The heavy baggage will be put on board from the same place, beginning on Monday morning. The 37th Regiment having for nearly three years formed a portion of this division of the army, the Major-General commanding is happy to bear testimony to the reputation it has maintained for orderly good conduct in quarters, as well as general attention to the rules of discipline and accuracy of movements in the field, and Sir Jeremiah Dickson offers to Major Skelly, the Officers and Soldiers of this efficient corps, his best wishes for their happy return to their native land.
(Signed) J. Bazalgette, D. A. A. General."

1842.

On the 17th January, 1842, the Regiment arrived at Cork.

1843.

On the 29th March, 1843, six Companies under command of Major Skelly crossed over to England, from Dublin, and disembarked at Bowness, whence they proceeded in detachments to Sunderland, Tynemouth, and Newcastle-upon-Tyne, the Head-Quarters and remaining Companies under command of Lieut.-Colonel Bradshaw, following a few days afterwards.

The entire embarkation of the Regiment at Dublin, and the subsequent arrival of the several Companies at their respective stations in England, occurred without one case of drunkenness among the men, or any casualty or irregularity taking place. This strong test of the good behaviour and soldier-like discipline of the corps so gratified the feelings of Sir Edward Blakeney, the Lieut.-General commanding Her Majesty's forces in Ireland, that he directed a minute of the same to be entered in the records of the Regiment.

On 12th July, new colours were presented to the Regiment by General the Honble. Sir G. A. Duff, G.C.B., who issued the following order on the occasion :-

"General Sir Alexander Duff cannot take leave of this Regiment without requesting Lieut.-Colonel Bradshaw to convey to the Officers, Non-Commissioned Officers, and Men of the 37th Regiment their Colonel's most unqualified approbation of the clean, soldier-like, and efficient bearing of the corps. It was with the highest satisfaction that from Head-Quarters, as well as from the Commander of the District, Major-General Brotherton, Sir Alexander Duff was led to believe that he would find his Regiment in the highest possible order; the observation that he has personally been enabled to make this day of the 37th has more than confirmed these previous favourable reports. Further, General Sir Alexander Duff cannot find words to express his approbation of the happy courtesy with which Lieut.-Colonel Bradshaw and his Officers unite the support of discipline with the most winning kindness to the Non-Commissioned Officers and Men, thereby ensuring the most cheerful and effective performance of duty.
Newcastle, Queen's Head Hotel, 12th July, 1843.

The following report of the Presentation of Colours appeared in the Newcastle Journal of 15th July :-

"New colours were presented on Wednesday last, with the permission of Field-Marshal His Grace the Duke of Wellington, to the 37th, or North Hampshire Regiment, now in garrison at Newcastle-upon-Tyne, Tynemouth, and Sunderland, and extensive preparations were made for the due observation of the interesting ceremony. The Head-Quarters of the Regiment and five Companies, under the command of Lieut-Colonel Bradshaw, are stationed at Newcastle, three Companies being stationed at Sunderland, under the command of Major Skelly, and two at Tynemouth, under the command of Major Francklyn. The Companies from Sunderland and Tynemouth arrived at the Head-Quarters of the Regiment at an early hour on Wednesday morning, having travelled by railway, and when on parade the Regiment comprised, in ten Companies, about eight hundred men. The ceremony was performed on the Town Moor, within the circle of the Race course nearly opposite the grand stand, and was witnessed by some hundreds of spectators. The ground which was nearly in the form of a square, having been "marked" in the usual way, was kept by a squadron of the 8th Royal Irish Hussars, under the command of Captain De Salis, who arrived about half-past eleven o'clock, and was followed shortly afterwards by a detachment of Royal Horse Artillery, with two field pieces, under the command of Lieut.-Col. Higgins, who took up a position near the south-west corner of the square. By this time a great number of ladies and gentlemen had assembled on the moor, and carriages were arriving in rapid succession, the latter drawing up in line to the north of the square, commanding an excellent view of the scene of action. Shortly before twelve o'clock, skirmishing parties were observed descending the hill, followed by their supports, and in a few minutes afterwards the main body of the regiment, in six companies, appeared on the heights, their bayonets glittering in the sun. They soon reached the ground and deployed into line, the left resting on the Race course near the grand stand, with the old regimental colours in the centre, the military band in two divisions, one at each end of the line, and the Royal Horse Artillery to the right. The new colours, about to be presented, were placed over the large drum, on the ground in front of the Regiment, which now awaited the arrival of the Inspecting Officer. But a few minutes elapsed, and then a flash of fire, followed by the loud report of the ordnance, indicated the approach of the General and his staff. General the Hon. Sir Alexander Duff, G.C.B., who has recently been appointed Colonel of the Regiment, reached Newcastle on the preceding day, accompanied by Lady Duff, and Miss Duff, and proceeded to the Queen's Head, from whence the General rode to the review, Lady and Miss Duff, who were also present on the occasion, and the former of whom, as will be seen, performed an important part in the interesting ceremony, having been conveyed in their carriage. The General and his suite were received with a salute of fifteen guns, fired by the artillerymen, whose expertness and precision excited general admiration; and on the General and his staff reaching the line, the band struck up the National Anthem, and the men presented arms, these honours due to the rank of the gallant General, being acknowledged by that veteran and distinguished officer, uncovered. The General then proceeded to inspect the men, and expressed himself highly pleased with the cleanliness and completeness exhibited in all their appointments. After the inspection of the line, the Regiment formed three sides of a square, in the centre of which the ceremony of consecrating the new colours was performed by the Rev. Wm. Dodd, M.A., incumbent of St Andrew's and principal chaplain to the garrison, in the presence of an immense number of ladies and gentlemen, who were permitted to enter the square, and thus had an excellent opportunity of witnessing the interesting proceedings. The minister, in a form of prayer provided, and in a very solemn and emphatic manner, consecrated the colours, praying that the Lord would bless them in the cause of truth, justice, religion, and piety, in defence of her Majesty the Queen, and in the maintenance of the constitution of these realms. A prayer for the Royal Family was next said; then the Lord's Prayer, after which the minister pronounced a benediction. The service having been concluded, the spectators retired to their former situation, and the Regiment again formed into line, when a royal salute was fired by the artillery, the ranks opened and the band trooped up. The ensigns bearing the old colours then took post in front of the grenadiers, who marched off as a guard to the tune of "The British Grenadiers," and halted near where the new colours were deposited, facing the line. Major Francklyn and Captain Harvey, the two senior officers next to the Lieut.Colonel, received the old colours from the Ensigns and delivered them to the Colour Sergeants. Sir Alexander Duff then handed them the new colours, which they carried to the Ensigns, who received them kneeling, the guns firing a royal salute, and the regiment presenting arms. The Majors then fell in, in front of the grenadiers, who marched away to the tune of "The Grenadiers' March," and on reaching the left of the line filed through the ranks, the colours halting on arriving at their place in the line, the escort moving on to their place on the right. The ranks were again closed, and the three sides of a square formed as before, Lieut.-Colonel Bradshaw being in the centre, when General the Hon. Sir Alex. Duff advanced, and addressed the regiment as follows :-

"Brother Soldiers and Companions in Arms,-It gives me high honour and satisfaction to meet you again, especially on this occasion, having obtained permission from his Grace Field-Marshal the Duke of Wellington, to present you with new colours. When I say I am happy to meet you again, you may not, perhaps, be aware that I had the honour of serving with you in 1794, in Holland, under his Royal Highness the Duke of York, and during that period I had frequent opportunities of witnessing the character and deportment of both Officers and men, and, I am proud to say, that for bravery, discipline, and general good conduct, the 37th is not surpassed by any Regiment in the service. I may refer particularly to the conduct of the Regiment near Nimeguen, as detailed in the Duke of York's despatches, and of which I was a witness. The advanced guard was suddenly attacked, and Lieutenant Wadman, who was on picquet, was shot through the head, his men being also sacrificed by the enemy. Notwithstanding the difficulties they had to encounter, the Regiment bore itself with distinguished bravery throughout the whole of that day, and I purpose handing over the record of their achievements to Colonel Bradshaw, in order that they may be more extensively known to those who may feel an interest in the subject. After this, the 37th were engaged with the 44th, the 57th, and 88th, in the West Indies, and I had the honour of serving in the same expedition, which embarked in 1795; but, as I hold in my hand a summary of the services of the Regiment, which I intend to read, I shall now content myself with thus generally alluding to services which have done you so much honour."

The gallant General then read a summary of the services of the Regiment, and Sir Alexander Duff concluded by stating, that he had searched the records of the Regiment, and it afforded him great satisfaction to find that it had uniformly distinguished itself in a very exemplary manner up to the present time, for Sir Edward Blakeney, Commander-in-Chief in Ireland, had inspected it on their recent departure from Ireland, and expressed the highest enconiums on the general good conduct and excellent discipline of the Regiment. He was proud to express the gratification their appearance had afforded him that day, and that Lieut.-Colonel Bradshaw and the Officers and men deserved great praise for their high state of discipline. He would take the old standards, which the Regiment had carried untarnished and with honour nearly forty years, and deposit them in his old castle by the side of the colours of another Regiment formerly under his command, the 92nd Highlanders, and preserve them as cherished monuments of their honour and exploits.

Lieut.-Colonel Bradshaw returned thanks for himself and on behalf of the Regiment, for the high honour paid them by the gallant General, and expressed his confidence that the new colours which he had presented to them would never be sullied. Then turning towards the Officers and men, the Lieutenant-Colonel said "Soldiers, I have said I trust our colours will never be sullied. Shall they ever?" "Never, never," was loudly responded from the ranks, and the greatest enthusiasm appeared to prevail, both amongst the Soldiers and the crowd of spectators assembled. Turning again towards the Officers and men of the Regiment, the Lieutenant-Colonel said, "Then let our Colonel hear what cheers we can give for our Queen-a Queen for whom we are prepared to shed the last drop of our blood, and whose rights we will ever defend. If you are ready to do all this, let me hear you say so." "We will, we will," was the unanimous response; in a moment every head was uncovered, and the air was rent with loud and protracted cheering. The colours were then lowered and decked with garlands of roses, and the spectators were again required to fall back, when the Regiment formed into line, advanced several paces, and then passed from the right of divisions in files to the rear. The regiment, preceded by the artillery, then marched past the General and his staff, in slow and quick time, after which they deployed into line."

1844.

During the summer of 1844, in consequence of a general strike among the colliers, detachments were sent to Durham and Thornley Hall.

In August of this year the Regiment moved to Manchester: thence in October to Gosport.

1845.

On the 7th April, 1845, the Regiment furnished a detachment for the Magazine at Marchwood, near Southampton, relieving the 49th Regiment in this duty. Towards the end of August, the Regiment under command of Lieut.-Colonel Honble. G. A. Spencer, proceeded into Monmouthshire and South Wales, relieving the 75th Regiment; the Head-Quarters were stationed at Newport.

1846.

In September, 1846, orders were received for the Regiment to hold itself in readiness to proceed to Ceylon, for which place it embarked at Gravesend with a strength of 1011 rank and file, still under the command of Lieut.-Colonel Spencer. They reached this island in March, 1847, and remained there for ten years.

1848.

Amongst other services in which the Regiment was engaged during this period, was the suppression of the revolt in the province of Kandy, in 1848.

1852.

In 1852, the Commanding Officer published in Regimental Orders the following letter from Lieut.-General Smelt, C.B., formerly Lieutenant-Colonel of the Regiment :

Colombo, Ceylon, April 3rd, 1852.
"My dear Colonel,-In the General Orders published to-day, I have announced the expiration of my service as Commander of the Forces in this Colony, and my concern in parting with the troops under my command. It is however with yet warmer feelings that I separate from a Regiment with which for so many years I have been intimately connected, and to which it has afforded me the greatest satisfaction to be again appointed as its Colonel.

The high character ever borne by the 37th Regiment, the perfect bearing of its Officers and the general good conduct of all ranks, have, I am proud to find, been continued since my early association with the corps, and contribute to mark a sound system of discipline, which assures me of its future career of distinction and honour. It is therefore with mingled feelings of pride and deep regret that I am about to bid adieu to the Regiment.

It was my hope to have been able to visit Kandy once again that I might have had the gratification of taking leave of the 37th Regiment in person, but the state of my health has prevented this, and I have, therefore, to request that you will have the goodness to communicate to the Officers, Non-Commissioned Officers, and men of the 37th, this my farewell, with my heartfelt wishes for their future happiness and welfare.
Believe me, Yours very faithfully,
(Signed) WM. SMELT, Lieut.-General, Colonel 37th Regiment.

Lieut.-Colonel Skelly, Commanding 37th Regiment, Kandy, Ceylon.
P. S.-I have to request that you will cause this letter to be preserved with the records of the Regiment.”

1857.

At the outbreak of the Indian Mutinies, in May, 1857, the Regiment received instructions to hold itself in readiness to proceed to Calcutta. The following Companies under command of Colonel Dames, Assistant Surgeons Fleming and Irwin in medical charge, were forthwith despatched, arriving in Calcutta on the 12th June, viz :-

Grenadiers.-Captain Harrison, Lieutenant Glass, Ensign Sale.
No. 2 Company. - Captain Bazalgette, Ensign Mason.
No. 7 Company. - Captain Jones, Lieutenant Bagenall.
No. 8 Company. - Captain Boissier, Ensign Fraser.
No. 9 Company. Lieutenant Jackson, Ensign Jones.
Light Company. Captain Clutterbuck, Lieutenant Reyne, Lieutenant Evered.

On the 14th of June, the above six Companies marched to "Cox's Bungalow," thirteen miles from Calcutta, on the Barrackpore road. Captain Clutterbuck died on the march from heat apoplexy.

About the end of the month, three of the above Companies (Nos. 2, 8, and 9), proceeded by Bullock train up the Grand Trunk Road to Benares, where a Company was afterwards detached to Chunar.

On the 8th July, the Grenadiers, No. 7, and Light Companies moved by steamer up the Ganges, and arrived at Dinapore on the 24th: here they disembarked to join a force composed of H.M's 10th Regiment and six guns of the Bengal Artillery in pursuit of the 7th, 8th, and 40th Regiments of Native Infantry, which had mutinied at Dinapore on that day.

The 37th under Captain Harrison, covered the guns and formed the skirmishing party in pursuit.

On the 29th July, these three Companies, amounting to 157 of all ranks, under Captain Harrison, with a detachment of 164 men of H.M's. 10th Regiment and 67 of Rattray's Sikhs, all being under the command of Captain Dunbar, H.M's. 10th Regiment, received orders to relieve some Europeans who were defending themselves in a fortified house at Arrah, where the mutineers had assembled in great force. The detachment, allowing themselves to continue their advance through the night, was suddenly surprised by the rebels in an ambuscade: a desperate encounter ensued: the fighting lasted till the following morning, the 30th; the regiment suffered severe loss on the occasion; three Officers, viz., Lieut. Bageneall, Lieut. Birkett, and Ensign Sale with 61 men were killed, and 24 men wounded. Captain Dunbar, who was in command, was himself killed. The survivors found their way back to Dinapore, and on the 4th August proceeded to Ghazeepore.

The Head-Quarters of the Regiment under command of Lieut.-Colonel Skelly, with a strength of 13 Officers, 19 Sergeants, 13 Corporals, 9 Drummers, 232 Privates, and 17 women and 38 children, left Ceylon in October; and on their arrival in Calcutta, were at once moved up to Dinapore; there they remained till the 25th March, 1858, when they proceeded to Ghazeepore.

Immediately on arrival at this station, a detachment under Captain Harrison, with Captain Luxmoore, Lieutenants, Savage, Henzell, and Fraser, were despatched to reinforce some Companies of the Regiment, which had been previously moved to Azimghur, under Colonel Dames.

On the 21st March, a force under Colonel E. M. Milman, 37th Regiment, composed of a detachment of the 37th Regiment, a Company of Madras Rifles, part of the 4th Madras Cavalry, and two guns, issued from Azimghur in the direction of Atrowlea, some forty miles distant, with the view of intercepting some mutineers, but in consequence of false information, found themselves confronted by a large army under Koer Singh: they withdrew without an engagement, falling back on Azimghur; the retreat was effected without the loss of a single man.

On the 24th, the Azimghur force was besieged by the whole of Koer Singh's Army. In a sortie which was made on the 27th, Captain Bedford and several men of the 37th were killed.

On the 15th of April the place was relieved by Sir Edward Lugard; upon which the men of the 37th under Captain Harrison, joined the Flying Column under Brigadier Douglas (now Sir John Douglas G.C.B.), in pursuit of Koer Singh, and were engaged with the rebels on the 17th and 20th, at Azmutghur and Sena Ghât.

In July a detachment under Captain Luxmoore, comprising 8 Officers, 12 Sergeants, 3 Drummers, and 202 Privates, joined a column commanded by Colonel Turner, which was operating in the Shahabad and Palamow district: they were present at the capture of Jugdespore, and several other engagements, in one of which Lieut. Henzell was wounded. The mutineers were pressed with great vigour, and their forces were completely scattered.

This detachment was employed in these operations until the 1st March, 1859, on which date they rejoined the Head-Quarters of the Regiment at Ghazeepore.

1860.

Early in 1860, the Regiment received orders to proceed to the Presidency: on the 2nd March, shortly before its departure, a calamitous fire occurred destroying a large portion of the men's barracks: to add to the general confusion and discomfort, cholera supervened. Quarter-Master Crutchley and many men, women, and children died during the ensuing fortnight: Calcutta was reached in May: the Regiment remained in Fort William until September, but in consequence of continued sickness, was moved to Hazareebagh, situate in the Rajmahal Hills, about 3000 feet above the sea.

On the 29th of October, a review by His Excellency the Commander in Chief, Sir Hugh Rose, called forth the following order. "Major Harrison has great pleasure in publishing to the Regiment, by orders of the Commander in Chief, His Excellency's gratification at the cleanliness and order of the barracks, and the appearance and steadiness of the Regiment on parade at his inspection yesterday. He also has signified his intention of making a most favourable report of the Regiment to H. R. H. the General Commanding in Chief."

1861.

In 1861, the Regiment, after giving 238 Volunteers to remain in India, proceeded by detachment to Calcutta, and embarked for England in two transports; the left-wing commanded by Major R. P. Harrison, in the "Matilda Atheling," on the 3rd March; the right-wing and Head-Quarters, under command of Major Sall, in the "Nimrod," on the 7th.

The whole Regiment disembarked at Gravesend in July, and took up their quarters at Devonport, in the Raglan Barracks, where Colonel Milman rejoined it in command.

1862.

In 1862, Major Francis Douglas Grey, exchanged from the 63rd Regiment with Major Harrison, and Major T. Young exchanged from the 22nd Regiment with Major H. M. Sall.

The Regiment proceeded to the camp at Aldershot in June.

Upon the creation of four Field-Marshals, in honour of the coming of age of H.R.H. The Prince of Wales, on the 9th November, Colonel Milman was promoted Major-General, and Major F. D. Grey succeeded to the command of the Regiment.

1864.

In August, 1864, the Regiment moved to Dover, and took up quarters in the Citadel. Sir John Pennefather having been struck with the good and clean order in which the huts vacated by the Regiment at Aldershot were left, brought the circumstance to the notice of H.R.H. the Duke of Cambridge, Commander-in-Chief, who was graciously pleased to express his satisfaction.

1865.

Early in 1865, the Depôt Companies from Pembroke Dock joined Head-Quarters in compliance with instructions received from the War Office. On Thursday, the 27th April, new Colours were presented to the Regiment, by the Secretary of State for War, Earl de Grey and Ripon: the Regiment paraded for this ceremony in an extensive meadow situate between Archcliff Fort and Shakespeare's Cliff. The old Colours, (or as they had come to be facetiously called "Colour Poles,") borne for the last time by Captain Collum and Captain Savage, were first "trooped," Captain Bazalgette commanding the escort.

The Regiment being formed into three sides of a square, the ceremony of consecration was emphatically performed by the Chaplain-General, Rev. G. R. Gleig, M.A. attended by the Rev. T. W. Burridge, and Rev. J. Y. Barton.

Earl de Grey and Ripon then advanced, and taking up the new Colours, presented them to Major T. Young and Major R. R. Pelly, who received them with a salute. His lordship thus addressed the Regiment :-

"Officers and Men of the 37th Regiment, I am proud to have been called upon on this occasion to present to this distinguished Regiment these new Colours, because the past history of this corps must assure everyone who has heard it that these Colours have been this day presented to men who are worthy to possess them. Raised at the commencement of the last century, the 37th took part in all the glorious campaigns of the great Duke of Marlborough. Your Regiment shared that wonderful march from Flanders to Bavaria, and took part in the crowning victory of Blenheim. You were present at Ramilies, and took your share of the victory of Oudenarde; and you did your part in the desperate struggle which resulted in the victory of Malplaquet. Afterwards when the English Army were called upon to defend the possessions of their sovereign and the interests of their country, you were found once more in the field and shared in the success of Dettingen. And again, when the Seven Years' War broke out, and England stood alone as the ally of the great King of Prussia, against the banded nations of Europe, you took part in that victory whose name is written first upon those Colours. There are some recollections connected with the name of Minden which Englishmen may think on with feelings of sorrow: but they must also think what was the conduct of those six battalions, of which you were one, to whom fell the brunt of that hard-fought field, whose loss was equal to half of that of the whole army of the Allies, who stood alone to secure the overthrow of your opponents. Again, when the war of the French Revolution heralded a foe for the arms of England, we find the 37th at the commencement of that struggle taking part on the plains of Tournay, and adding another name to those recorded on your banners. It was not your fortune to have so large a share as some Regiments in the successes of the Peninsula, because you were employed in discharging a less exciting though not less necessary duty, in defending the fortresses or the colonies of your country. But before that war closed you joined the army with England's greatest captain, and won again to your honoured colours the glorious name "Peninsula." And so it has been until that latest period when you went forth from the shores of England to meet another enemy upon the plains of India. There where was the small British army, standing against a multitude of fierce insurgents, resisting, and overthrowing that wicked rebellion-you again were found foremost among the victorious battalions of England, at the defence of Azimghur, and the battle of Koelsa, which entitled you once more to the gratitude of your sovereign and your country. Thus it has been through your history for 160 years, that whenever called upon you have been ready, and have done nobly the duty that has been claimed from you-and to have nobly done his duty is the highest honour to the British soldier! Men of the 37th, can we doubt on looking back upon that honourable career, that this Regiment will ever maintain the reputation thus nobly won? I doubt not there are many among you who will see with regret those tattered banners, torn by the shot of the enemy and darkened by the smoke of the battle, borne away from the ranks in which you have so long preserved and defended them. But the memory of your past actions will not pass away with them: these recollections will continue to hover round the colours now entrusted to you, Thirty-seventh! take these new standards; defend them on other and future fields. These colours are hallowed by the rites of religion, and entrusted to you by your Queen to guard them, Officers and Men of the 37th, as a sacred deposit, dearer than your lives! see that they are not tarnished while they are in your keeping; and take care when they are flung out on the morning of some future battle, you so guard and so bear them that before they are again furled in the evening, you have added another glorious name to the long roll of British victories."

Upon this the new Colours were lowered while the Colonel's wife encircled each with a garland of choice flowers: they were handed to Ensign Graham Bowles Dobson and Ensign Frederick Flowers; who received them kneeling, the whole Regiment presenting arms.

Lieut. Colonel Grey then rode forward and said :-

"My Lord-I cannot let the ceremony which has brought us all here, and to the success of which your Lordship has so greatly contributed by your presence and kindness in taking so prominent a part in it, come to a conclusion, without attempting some reply to your Lordship's flattering address, or without assuring your Lordship that the honest and hearty thanks of a gratified soldiery are yours, for the service you have so courteously rendered them this day. Soldiers are proverbially but poor orators, and I can claim for myself no exception to the general rule. Nevertheless I would fain believe that the thanks of a soldier simply expressed are no whit inferior in value to-nay, as a rule, are of more worth than those couched in the lofty strains of a polished rhetorician. At a time like the present, however, one might well wish for the power to make fitting reply. The occasion, the scene, are no ordinary ones. We stand upon a spot of perhaps more historic interest than attaches to any other in this land, where was fought the first battle recorded in our country's history; and which from its proximity to the neighbouring continent, has ever since been, and must ever continue to be, of the utmost military importance-an importance that has not been sufficiently recognised by the general public; but which, fortunately for the same public, has not escaped the notice of our military rulers. It is certain that the heights above us, well fortified and manned, preclude the idea of a second invasion at this spot. Indeed, the strategical value of the position is such as to make a sane man, having a communication to preserve with the opposite shore, hesitate before throwing his forces on any part of the coast. On this spot, then, so appropriate to the occasion, stands the British Regiment, whose career your lordship has now so graphically painted, to receive, and having just received, at your Lordship's hands, in token of the continuance of the trust reposed by the nation in its arms, new colours. Surrounding us throng England's free children to witness, and by their presence add to, the honour now conferred on those whose task is their freedom, whose meed their approbation; whose pride it is this day to surrender unsullied their well-worn colours; whose duty it will be to command like fortune for their new ones. I say advisedly "command fortune." It is but to deserve, to have. And who can doubt that the spirit of our forefathers is still here the spirit that at Minden urged those six weak Regiments onward, ever onward-slowly 'tis true, and little wonder, but as surely as the advance of time itself, through the hosts of the enemy, the chivalry of France-that spirit which at later periods, and under no less trying circumstances, has so frequently impelled its votaries to victory, scorning danger, courting death. Here, too, I would have your Lordship believe is another and yet more admirable characteristic of the soldier of bygone times-discipline; without which all the pluck in the world, when opposed to organized resistance, is valueless, but with which and controlled by which success is insured-aye, if it be but success in defeat; for there is a success to be had even in defeat. When, on the 19th October, 1794, this Regiment furnished the outposts for our army in Holland, it had to bear the brunt of the enemy's attack, which he made with 30,000 men. The most advanced post, found by the Light Company, was sacrificed, and the Regiment itself overwhelmed. Yet was it successful! Successful not only in that by its constancy, and gallantry it gave the army it covered time to prepare for, and thereby repel with triumph, the enemy's attack; but in that, above all, it proved to demonstration that no defeat, that not death itself, has power to rob the individual of his honour. The day, the cause even, may be lost, but the soldier, if he be worthy of the name, remains indomitable. This affair of Druten is only one of numerous instances of a similar kind! but it amply suffices to prove the justice of my assertion that we may "command fortune." And if we may command fortune it follows that we must. This is our plain duty, and as such we gladly accept it; and while as christians we sincerely desire a continuance of peace, as soldiers, should war come upon us-of which there appears at the present time a more than ordinary probability-we shall as heartily long for the opportunity to add to the laurels bequeathed to us by our predecessors; and by the aid of Him to whose service our banners have been dedicated, we shall not be disappointed."

The ceremony being over, the Regiment reformed line under the new Colours. The old Colours, preceded by the band playing "Auld Lang Syne," were borne along the ranks for the last time, each Company in turn presenting arms as they passed. At a signal from Lieut.-Colonel Grey, the Officers and men gave three hearty and lengthened cheers as a farewell tribute of honour to the old Colours.

On the 19th May the old Colours were deposited with fitting ceremonial in All Saints Church at Aldershot;* and in connexion with this occasion took place the formal opening of the Memorial East Window, which had been erected in that Church by the Regiment :-

"This window, designed and executed by Messrs. O'Connor, is about 30 feet high and 15 feet wide, and consists of five main lights and a large tracery. The general "motif" intended to be conveyed, is that of the sorrows of war, and the triumph of victory, illustrated by Old Testament history in the outer lights, and further shewn in the great centre lights by our Lord's own victory over Sin and Death, and in the tracery by the glorious Resurrection of "All Saints"- the dedication of the Church. Appropriately, therefore, to so sad, yet glorious a memorial, the subjects selected by the artists shew in each of the side lights a painful struggle and a great success. Beginning from the north, the subject is the Children of Israel, under Joshua, repulsed before Ai with slaughter, some staying by the way to mourn over the dead. Again, under the same leader, they vanquish the city, and he offers sacrifice to the God of Armies. Under these two subjects are the words "As I was with Moses, so will I be with thee." In the last two of the five openings the Angel of the Lord is represented as appearing to Gideon, the bravest of warriors. He is secretly at work threshing corn in a wine-press, when the Angel appears and announces to him that he is to be the deliverer of Israel from the Midianites, and gives him a sign by fire from heaven. With a small band of 300 men, and by a warlike stratagem, he gains a great conquest. Under these two subjects is written his war-cry, suggestive of Faith and Humility, "The Sword of the Lord and of Gideon." All these groups are placed on each side of the great centre subject, "The Resurrection of Christ," under which are the words, "I am the Resurrection and the Life;" these groups are all enshrined within rich Mosaic jewelled canopies, running up with a ground-work of bold grisaille diapered glass, upon which are laid bosses of colour of various devices, and quatrefoil opes, containing groups of Angels' trumpets, the border being designed of the Rose and the Crown. The tracery has in the two main quatrefoil opes over the outer lights representations of the Archangels Raphael and Gabriel; in the great rose of the tracery is St. Michael, the Archangel, trampling upon the serpent; and above that is our Lord seated in majesty, the King of Saints, while every other opening is filled with the figures of "All Saints." In a series of panels extending along the base of the five opes the 37th Regiment is more specially commemorated: in the centre by an arrangement of the two colours of the Regiment, painted on the glass, the Royal colour being placed on the dexter side, and the Regimental colour on the sinister side; next to these are monograms of the initials of the Regiment-N.H.R.-its number, &c., surmounted by the Crown, the Rose, Shamrock, and Thistle, &c. The memorial inscription at the foot of the window painted on the glass, in very legible letters, is "To the Glory of God, and in Memory of all the Officers, Non-commissioned Officers, and Private Soldiers, of the 37th (North Hampshire) Regiment who were killed, or died while serving in India during the Mutiny, from 1857 to 1859: Also in Honour of the Colours here deposited, under which they served; this Window was erected by all ranks serving in the Regt., A.D. 1865 assisted by other of the Survivors."

* The old Colours of the 62nd Regiment were deposited at the same time.

In June the Regiment proceeded to Ireland and took up quarters in the Curragh Camp.

On the 13th December, under command of Lieut.-Colonel Grey they were suddenly ordered to Cork in aid of the Civil power during the sitting of a Special Commission on Fenian prisoners.

1866.

On the 18th January, 1866, the Head-Quarters marched by road to Fermoy: three Companies having previously been sent on detachment to Tralee: two Companies were afterwards sent to Lismore, and one to Mitchelstown.

On Lieut.-Colonel Grey's retirement in March, the following Regimental Order was issued.

"Fermoy, March 25th, 1866.
"Lieut.-Colonel Grey cannot resign the command of the Regiment without thanking all grades for the support they have cheerfully and continually given him, or without assuring them of the lasting interest with which he will ever regard the career of the Regiment."

At the same time Surgeon-Major F. W. Fleming, F.R.C.S., was transferred from the Regiment to take over medical charge of the 4th Dragoon Guards. The mention of this Officer's name will at once recall to the memory of all who knew him the most cherished feelings of esteem and regret. For seventeen years he devoted himself to the welfare of the Regiment. By his example and persuasive admonition he imparted both to Officers and Men a high moral tone and an enthusiasm for the duties of their profession.

On the 9th May, orders were received for the Regiment to hold itself in readiness to proceed to Bengal.

Two Companies under Captain Davis remained at home to form the Depôt, and joined the 9th Depôt Battalion at Preston.

On the 6th July, the left-wing under command of Major Savage, embarked at Queenstown, in the transport ship "Middlesex" for Calcutta, which they reached on the 24th October.

The Head-Quarters under Lieut.-Colonel Bazalgette, who had succeeded Lieut.-Colonel Grey in the command of the Regiment, followed a week later on board the "Blenheim," arriving in Calcutta on the 29th October.

The Regiment having assembled at Barrackpore, moved in two detachments to Cawnpore by railway: thence it proceeded by regular marches under command of Lieut.-Colonel Bazalgette, to Bareilly, where it remained for two years.

The Rohilcund District in which Bareilly is situate, was at this time commanded by Brigadier-General Percy Hill, C.B.

1867.

On the 14th May, 1867, the Paymaster, Captain Budgen, died of cholera after a few hours' illness. Captain Budgen had joined the Regiment since its arrival in India, and by his open-hearted kindness had endeared himself to all who knew him. A monument raised by the Officers to his memory marks his grave in the cemetery at Bareilly.

1868.

On the 21st November, 1868, the Regiment moved from Bareilly, the Head-Quarters under Lieut.-Colonel J. Davis, who had succeeded to the command in December, 1867, marching to Shahjehanpore, the left-wing under Major Savage to Moradabad. The Rohilcund District was now under command of Brigadier General Macdonell, C.B.

In the cemetery at Shahjehanpore a monument has been erected by the Officers to the memory of Captain Graham Bowles Dobson, who died on 13th December, 1868, from injuries sustained by a fall from his horse.

1869.

The following year was marked by the loss of another Officer, which called forth the following Regimental Order :-

"Shahjehanpore, December 5th, 1869.
"It is with extreme regret that Lieut.-Colonel Davis has to announce to the Regiment the death of Major Savage, which occurred at Moradabad, on the morning of the 3rd instant, after a short illness.

"By the kindness of his disposition, Major Savage gained the esteem of all with whom he was brought in contact, and Lieut.-Colonel Davis feels assured that all ranks will unite with him in deploring the loss the Regiment has sustained by the death of one in whom were combined the happy qualities of a good soldier and a thorough gentleman."

1870.

In March, 1870, the Regiment was inspected by Brigadier-General A. Macdonell, C.B., when the following District Order was published :-

"The Brigadier-General has much pleasure in conveying to Lieut.-Colonel Davis his satisfaction at the result of the inspection of the Regiment under his command for the first period of 1870."

At this time a working party consisting of 200 men, under Captains King and Cochran proceeded to Raneekhet for the purpose of making roads, and was thus employed till the end of the year.

In accordance with an order from the War Office dated the 19th June, the establishment of the Regiment was reduced by two companies.

In November the Regiment marched from Shahjehanpore en route to Meeanmeer, passing through Bareilly and Moradabad, where the left wing rejoined Head Quarters.

They were here inspected by His Excellency The Right Honourable The Commander in Chief, Lord Napier of Magdala, who expressed himself well satisfied with the efficient state of the Regiment.

The following District Order by Brigadier General A. Macdonell, C.B., was received when the Regiment was leaving his command :-

"Head-Quarters, Bareilly, November 17th, 1870.
"The Brigadier-General commanding is particularly desirous that the 37th Foot should not leave the district without his expression of regret at parting with the corps.

"The good conduct, appearance, and efficiency of the Regiment are qualities which the Brigadier-General has watched with interest in their gradual development by the united solicitude of Lieut.-Colonel Davis, admirably seconded by Officers, Non-commissioned Officers, and Men. The Regiment having marched to Meerut, proceeded by rail in two detachments to Meean Meer, where they relieved the 85th Light Infantry.

It remained in this station two years and four months, furnishing a Company monthly to the Fort at Lahore.

1872.

In August, 1872, the Regiment was visited by a severe outbreak of cholera, resulting in a total loss of 67 men, 7 women, and 26 children.

Every effort was made to check the ravages of the epidemic by dispersing the troops.

Upon its subsidence, the Regiment re-united at Meean Meer, whence it was afterwards moved to the hill station of Dugshai.

1873-75.

In the early part of 1873, Colonel R. H. Browne having exchanged from the XX Regiment with Colonel Davis, took over command, which he retained for nearly two years. Upon the Regiment leaving India, however, he exchanged with Colonel V. H. Bowles from the 63rd Regiment.

In November, 1874, the left wing under Major Douglas Alleyne, left Dugshai, proceeded by rail from Umballa to Bombay, and landing in England on the 21st January, 1875, took up quarters at Gosport.

The Head-Quarters marched from Umballa through Delhi, where Colonel Bowles took command, to Cawnpore and Allahabad: thence they proceeded by rail to Bombay, and arrived in England on the 5th April.

1876.

On the 13th April, 1876, a detachment of four Companies under Major Alleyne, proceeded to Portland, and occupied the Verne Citadel, until the arrival of the 31st Regiment from Gibraltar, on the 4th May, when the detachment rejoined Head-Quarters at Gosport.

1877.

Early in 1877, Colonel Bowles retired on half-pay, and Lieut.-Colonel Glass succeeded to the command of the Regiment.

In March, the 37th moved to Winchester, the capital of their own County; a small detachment was supplied for the Magazine at Marchwood.

The following Regimental Order bears testimony to the universal regret with which, in consequence of the new Medical Regulations, the Regiment at this time felt the loss of their Surgeon Major :-

"Winchester, March 16th, 1877. "The Commanding Officer has to inform the Regiment that they are about to be deprived of the services of Surgeon-Major Ekin, M.B., who has been ordered to return to Gosport, for duty on the staff. During the eight years which Surgeon-Major Ekin belonged to the Regiment, there are few individuals who have not benefited by his advice and experience. His devoted energy and cheerful bearing during the trials of the fatal cholera season at Meean Meer can never be forgotten. Both socially and professionally his departure will be deeply felt by all ranks, as he has always had at heart the interest and welfare of the Regiment. He carries with him the good wishes of all."

A few words about the Depôt Companies :

As has been previously stated, the system of four Company Depôts was established in 1825: this was changed in 1855 : and Depôt Battalions were formed of two Companies from several Regiments, called Depôt Companies :-Those of the 37th were quartered first at Chatham, afterwards at Chichester, thence they moved to Colchester, and finally to Pembroke Dock, where they remained till they joined the Head-Quarters of the Regiment in 1865.

On the embarkation of the Regiment for India in 1866, two Companies remained behind at the Depôt, and were sent to Preston, afterwards to Pembroke Dock :-On the 18th March, 1870, the Depôt Battalion system was discontinued, the Depôt Companies of the 37th were affiliated first to the 80th Regiment during the time that that Regiment was quartered at Birr and Belfast, and afterwards to the 15th Regiment, with whom they served at the Curragh Camp, at Dublin, and at Cork. They were brought over to Portsmouth in December, 1872, and were attached for a few months to the 104th Fusiliers.

On the 1st April, 1873, under the new system for localising Regiments, and closely connecting them with their respective counties, the Depôt Companies of the 37th in conjunction with those of the 67th Regiment formed the 40th Brigade Depôt under the command of Colonel Thomas, C.B. (late 67th Regiment): at present they occupy Fort Elson, near Gosport, pending their eventual removal to Winchester.

Thus closes up to the present time the record of a Regiment whose annals are not ingloriously associated with the history of the nation during the two centuries which have elapsed since its first formation, and of whose future it is not too much to predict that it shall never dim the lustre of its past.
